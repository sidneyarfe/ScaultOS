# Patterns — Breakthrough Advertising Techniques

## The 5 Stages of Awareness (headline strategy)
**When to use**: diagnosing what a headline is allowed to say.
**How**: Most Aware → name + price. Product-Aware → sharpen/extend/reinforce the known claim (7 sub-jobs). Solution-Aware → name the desire + prove achievable + connect to your product. Problem-Aware → name the problem, crystallize into the need, then connect. Completely Unaware → identification headline only, no price/name/direct claim.
**Trade-offs**: matching the wrong stage either bores an advanced market or confuses/loses an unaware one. Requires honest market research, not assumption.

## The 5 Stages of Market Sophistication (claim strategy)
**When to use**: deciding whether to state the claim directly, escalate it, or lead with mechanism.
**How**: 1st (first in market) → state claim simply/directly. 2nd → escalate/enlarge the claim. 3rd → claim exhausted, introduce new mechanism in headline. 4th → competitors copy your mechanism, you elaborate it further. 5th (exhausted) → find a genuinely new mechanism or switch to pure identification.
**Trade-offs**: escalation (2nd/4th) has a believability ceiling; pushing past it triggers skepticism and regulatory scrutiny. Skipping straight to mechanism (3rd) in a 1st-stage market wastes the novelty of the raw claim.

## Intensification (13-technique desire-building toolkit)
**When to use**: expanding an already-chosen desire into full body copy.
**How**: direct description → product-in-action → reader-inclusion → testability → time-stretch → audience/testimonial → expert approval → compare/contrast → before/after (black side) → ease-of-benefit → metaphor → catalog summary → guarantee reinforcement. Apply as many as give a genuinely fresh angle; stop when a new paragraph would just repeat the prior one.
**Trade-offs**: overuse without fresh angles reads as padding; underuse leaves desire under-built for a cold/skeptical market.

## Identification (character vs. achievement roles)
**When to use**: when the product's functional benefit alone isn't enough to differentiate, or when buyers need a self-image reason to choose you.
**How**: identify the product's already-believable primary image. If favorable, intensify it (stack reinforcing symbols on the same trait). If unfavorable/neutral, subordinate it and bridge to a bigger, believable target image — never contradict the primary image outright.
**Trade-offs**: an identification image that fails the believability-bridge test (not believed to have the trait, or not connectable to the reader's life) collapses trust in the *whole* ad, not just that one claim.

## Gradualization (belief architecture)
**When to use**: any claim strong/surprising enough that stating it cold would be rejected.
**How**: open with a statement earning instant, effortless acceptance (inclusion question or detailed identification). Chain each subsequent statement onto the prior accepted one (contingency structures, logic-language, paragraph parallelism, syllogisms). Only state the goal conclusion once enough acceptance is banked.
**Trade-offs**: skipping preparation to "get to the point" faster usually produces a *weaker* ad, even though it's shorter — position multiplies content-strength.

## Redefinition (3 objections → 3 fixes)
**When to use**: product has a built-in handicap (complexity, low perceived importance, high price) actively repelling prospects.
**How**: too complicated → Simplification (name the smallest actual unit of effort). Not important enough → Escalation (widen the product's role in the reader's life). Costs too much → Price Reduction (establish a credible higher anchor first, then reveal the real price against it).
**Trade-offs**: using the wrong technique for the actual objection (e.g. escalating importance when the real block is complexity) leaves the true objection unresolved.

## Mechanization (3-depth reason-why)
**When to use**: whenever a claim triggers a silent "how does it work?" — which is almost always.
**How**: Name the mechanism (market already accepts it) → Describe it (Promise then Reason-Why, in selling language) → Feature it in the headline (market sophistication has exhausted the raw promise).
**Trade-offs**: over-explaining a familiar mechanism bores an already-convinced reader; under-explaining an unfamiliar one leaves the claim unbelieved.

## Concentration (paired attack + fix)
**When to use**: breaking existing brand loyalty, or protecting your own loyalty in a competitive category.
**How**: never state a competitor/alternative weakness without immediately pairing it with your product's matching strength (point-by-point Bad→Good structure), OR narrate the reader's own past-failure history with the old solution before revealing your active mechanism as the resolution (before/after time-sequence structure).
**Trade-offs**: attack without a paired fix reads as pure disparagement and increases skepticism instead of reducing it. Skip Concentration entirely if you dominate the category (silence protects loyalty better) or your offer is different enough that naming rivals only advertises them.

## Camouflage (3 believability-borrowing levers)
**When to use**: audience has strong trust habits tied to a specific medium/format, or is skeptical of standard "advertising language."
**How**: Format — mirror the publication's actual typographic conventions. Phraseology — borrow the medium's stock language (news dateline, reporting tone). Mood — Understatement (short sentences, few adjectives, self-kidding) or Deadly Sincerity (admit a real flaw before claiming a benefit).
**Trade-offs**: format-matching requires medium-specific rework per placement — not a set-and-forget tactic. Deadly Sincerity only works on a genuinely minor flaw; overusing it on major weaknesses undermines the product.

## Interweaving (sentence-level density)
**When to use**: every sentence of finished copy, as a final craft pass.
**How**: check each sentence for promise + image + logic simultaneously present, not spread across separate sentences. Combine a mood-builder, an authority-reference, a core promise, and a proof-echo in one line where possible.
**Trade-offs**: over-packed sentences can become unreadable; the goal is density that still reads smoothly, verified by reading aloud.

## Sensitivity (whole-ad architecture)
**When to use**: when a draft feels "stuck" or the reader would plausibly want a different kind of material next.
**How**: mark each section as promise-heavy, documentation-heavy, or blended. Diagnose which dimension (desire/identification/belief) the reader is currently under-served on, and reorder or reweight existing material toward it — before writing new content.
**Trade-offs**: no single "correct" architecture exists — Schwartz shows 4 valid structures for the same product category, chosen by each ad's specific reader, not by formula.

## Momentum (forward-pull devices)
**When to use**: any copy long enough to risk the reader dropping off mid-read.
**How**: insert explicit transition phrases ("Here's why," "For example—") at natural pause points, and deliberately incomplete statements that create a mini-curiosity-gap ("The attachment that HAD TO BE BANNED... because...").
**Trade-offs**: momentum devices that don't also advance the sale are just filler — every one should simultaneously build desire/belief, not merely keep eyes moving.
