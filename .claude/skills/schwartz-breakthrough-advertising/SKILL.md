## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

Schwartz's central claim: **copy does not create desire — it channels desire that already exists.** Every technique below is a tool for finding that existing desire and directing it at a product, never for inventing want from nothing.

### The Diagnostic Sequence (do this before writing anything)
1. **Mass Desire** (Ch 1) — what does a large-enough group of people already want badly enough to pay for? Score candidates on urgency × staying-power × scope. Pick ONE dominant desire per ad.
2. **State of Awareness** (Ch 2) — how much does THIS reader already know about how my product satisfies that desire? Five walls, not a gradient: Most Aware → Product-Aware → Solution-Aware → Problem-Aware → Completely Unaware. The headline must start exactly where the reader's mind is — never assume they're further along than they are.
3. **Market Sophistication** (Ch 3) — how many competitors have already made this claim? Five stages: 1st (state it plainly) → 2nd (escalate it) → 3rd (claim exhausted, lead with a NEW MECHANISM) → 4th (mechanism gets elaborated) → 5th (exhausted — switch to pure identification, no claim at all).

Awareness and sophistication are independent axes — diagnose both. A market can know your brand (Product-Aware) while being numb to every claim in the category (5th-stage sophisticated); that combination needs a fresh mechanism, not a louder promise.

### Verbalization (Ch 4-5)
Once the strategic content is fixed, wording is a separate craft pass: measure the claim (size/speed/comparison), sensory-charge it, prove it with a vivid demonstration, reframe as paradox/question/challenge. Never build a headline by copying a competitor's proven formula word-for-word ("echo ads") — analysis from scratch beats formula-application beats word-substitution, always. Give a product ONE dominant personality trait and let every other trait reinforce it.

### The Three Dimensions of the Reader's Mind (Ch 6)
Every persuasion technique targets one of three levers. Diagnose the weakest one before deciding what the copy needs:
- **Desire** (physical/material want) → toolkit: **Intensification**
- **Identification** (self-image/role the product confers) → toolkit: **Identification**
- **Belief** (existing map of what's true/safe) → toolkit: **Gradualization, Redefinition, Mechanization, Concentration**

### The Seven Techniques of Breakthrough Copy (Ch 7-13)
1. **Intensification** — build desire by giving continually fresh angles on the SAME promise (13 sub-techniques: direct description, product-in-action, reader-inclusion, testability, time-stretch, testimonial, expert approval, compare/contrast, before/after, ease-of-benefit, metaphor, catalog summary, guarantee).
2. **Identification** — build a saleable self-image into the product via character roles (unstated, unconscious — "chic," "brilliant") or achievement roles (visible status markers — "Executive"). Never contradict the product's existing believable image; intensify it if favorable, bridge from it if not.
3. **Gradualization** — architect belief: open with a statement earning instant acceptance, chain each next statement onto the prior one, only state the real claim (the "goal conclusion") once enough acceptance is banked. One fully-believed promise outsells ten partially-believed ones.
4. **Redefinition** — reframe a built-in objection: too-complicated → Simplification (name the smallest real unit of effort); not-important → Escalation (widen the product's role in the reader's life); too-expensive → Price Reduction (state a credible higher anchor before the real price).
5. **Mechanization** — verbal proof of "how it works," in three depths matched to market awareness: name it (already accepted) → describe it (Promise then Reason-Why) → feature it in the headline (promise itself is exhausted, stage-3 sophistication).
6. **Concentration** — direct attack on competitors/alternatives, ALWAYS paired with your fix for the named weakness in the same breath. Bare disparagement backfires; attack-plus-solution reads as a service to the reader.
7. **Camouflage** — borrow believability from the trusted medium: match its Format (typographic conventions), Phraseology (news dateline, reporting tone), or Mood (Understatement — short/plain/self-kidding, or Deadly Sincerity — admit a real flaw before claiming a benefit).

### Final Touches (Ch 14)
Verification (place proof exactly where the reader is already demanding it) · Reinforcement (juxtapose two modest claims for 1+1=10 impact) · Interweaving (pack promise+image+logic into the same sentence) · Sensitivity (read your own draft as the reader, reorder before you rewrite) · Momentum (transition phrases + incomplete-statement hooks) · Mood (word/rhythm choice as an invisible emotional channel).

**Governing rule across the whole book**: "No formula works twice." Every technique above is a diagnostic lens for THIS product/market/moment, never a template to paste in. When in doubt, re-run the diagnostic sequence rather than reaching for a formula.
