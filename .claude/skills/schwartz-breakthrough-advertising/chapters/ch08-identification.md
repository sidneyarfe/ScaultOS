# Chapter 8: The Second Technique — Identification

## Core Idea
Beyond physical satisfaction, every product can offer the buyer a *role* — a way to define themselves to the world. Identification is the technique of building a saleable personality into the product so that buying it also buys the buyer a piece of self-image.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Two Kinds of Roles**:
  1. **Character roles** — adjectives-turned-identity (progressive, chic, brilliant, well-read). Subjective, largely unconscious, never openly claimed — because they live in fantasy and are never fact-checked by the buyer, they are the *easiest* type of claim to get accepted. A product can serve a character role three ways: help achieve it, help simplify/speed achieving it, or symbolize it to others.
  2. **Achievement roles** — nouns-as-titles (Executive, Homeowner, Career Woman) awarded by society for status attained. Because achievement is invisible without a physical marker, buyers use *products* to display it — "in America today we are known not only by the company we keep but by the products we own."
- **The Primary Image**: every product already carries an existing, believable image in the prospect's mind (a piston ring is precision-made; a cigarette carries inherited virility) whether or not that image is useful. Two moves: **intensify** the primary image if it's favorable (stack multiple reinforcing symbols onto the same trait, e.g. Marlboro layering virile men + rugged occupations + tattoos, all pointing at the same virility image), or **subordinate-and-bridge** it if it's negative/neutral — never contradict it outright, because "people just won't believe that a product is what it isn't." Fold the old image in as a smaller, believable element, then use it as a bridge to a broader, more desirable image built around it.
- **The Believability-Bridge test** (2 questions before using any identification image): (1) Do people already believe the product *has* this trait? (2) Can they connect that trait to their *own* life or aspiration? If either answer is No, don't state the target image directly — insert a first-accepted image the reader already believes, and use it as the hook the target image hangs from.

## Key Concepts
- **Saleable identifications from the physical product itself**: three under-used sources of image material — **appearance** (borrow design cues from the prestige end of the category, e.g. give a family sedan the fin/hubcap details of a racing car), **components** (a product with electronic parts should look and read electronic — borrow from radar/aerospace imagery), and **background/history** (Johnson & Johnson's Micrin mouthwash borrowed the physical shape of hospital-grade glass containers, so the package itself said "medicine" before a word of copy was read).
- **Multi-image symbols**: a single visual can carry several identifications at once — owning a fine painting signals success *and* cultural refinement *and* intellectual accomplishment simultaneously, broadening the market while deepening the pull for each individual buyer.

## Mental Models
- Never try to erase a product's existing image and replace it wholesale — that triggers disbelief which bleeds into distrust of every other claim in the ad. Instead, ask: "what is this product's unavoidable primary image, and what more valuable image can I attach to it *without contradicting it*?"
- When constructing an identification scene (people, setting, objects), work backward from the target role: what possessions/companions/settings would a person who has *already achieved* that role surround themselves with? Populate the ad with those specific signals rather than a generic "aspirational" mood.

## Worked Example
Schwartz's from-scratch build for a hypothetical piston-ring ad, escalating identification step by step: the primary image (mechanical, precision-made, replaced only when there's trouble) is neutral-to-negative. Rather than fight it, the copy **upgrades every element around it**: the car becomes a $12,000 Mercedes SL-300 (success + excitement), the garage becomes a spotless sports-car shop (precision, professionalism), the mechanic is mature and exacting (expertise), the owner is casually confident (achieved success worn lightly), and the ring-replacement itself is reframed not as "something broke" but as proactive performance-tuning — "installing high-performance equipment, exactly as he would install a supercharger." Every visual choice reinforces the same target identification (precision + achieved success) while never contradicting the product's inherently mechanical, unglamorous primary image.

## Key Takeaways
1. Every product carries an unavoidable primary image — work with it, never against it.
2. Character-role claims work best *unstated* — imply through symbol (setting, occupation, object), never through direct adjectives, because unstated claims bypass conscious fact-checking.
3. Achievement-role buyers need *visible* markers — the product must be able to be *seen* by others to do its identification job.
4. If the target image isn't already believable and connected to the reader's life, insert a believability-bridge image first — don't jump straight to the aspirational claim.
5. Mine the product's own physical appearance, components, and background for identification material before inventing anything external.
