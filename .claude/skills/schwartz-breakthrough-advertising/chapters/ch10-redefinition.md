# Chapter 10: The Fourth Technique — Redefinition

## Core Idea
Every product carries at least one built-in handicap that repels prospects before the sale even starts. Redefinition removes the objection — not by hiding it, but by reframing the facts around it so the objection no longer applies, often *before the reader even consciously registers it existed*.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **3 Categories of Drawback, 3 Matching Techniques**:
  1. **Too complicated / too hard to use** → **Simplification**. Redefine the feared process into something small and familiar. Never claim "it's easy" directly — *prove* it by reframing the vocabulary itself (Sherwin Cody's English course: "only 15 minutes a day," reframing all of English grammar into "69 words that make up half of all speech," "one dozen punctuation principles," "25 typical errors" — the field itself gets redefined as small).
  2. **Not important enough** → **Escalation**. Broaden the product's role from a narrow benefit into a bigger, more central one — spark plugs redefined from "a 99¢ part" into "the thing standing between you and the full power/enjoyment of a $4,000 car"; an English course redefined from correctness-for-its-own-sake into "the persuasion tool you use in every waking minute of your life."
  3. **Costs too much** → **Price Reduction (psychological)**. Don't argue the price is fair — *change the comparison anchor* the reader measures it against. Robert Collier's technique: state what the product "would have to sell for" (a higher anchor, made credible with a specific backstory — overstocked mill, S.O.S. call, quarter-million yards), *then* reveal the real, lower price against that anchor. The felt discount comes entirely from which number the reader compares against, not from the price itself changing.
  - When to use: diagnose which of the 3 objections is actually blocking the sale before writing — simplification, escalation, and price-reduction language are not interchangeable; using the wrong one wastes the redefinition entirely.

## Key Concepts
- **The turn**: the delicate transition point where copy moves from "you fear X" to "actually X is redefined as Y" — must happen without a jar. Schwartz's example: a TV-repair ad never uses the word "repairs" until it has first redefined the concept as "symptoms" and "adjustments," borrowing the human-body-warning-signal metaphor so the reader accepts the reframe before the scary word ever appears.
- **Redefinition ≠ lying**: the underlying facts don't change — only which facts get emphasized, and what vocabulary frames them, changes. "Repairs" become "5-minute adjustments"; "$40 wholesale cost" becomes "would have sold for $5 each."

## Mental Models
- Before writing simplification copy, ask: "what is the *actual smallest unit* of effort this product requires, and can I name that unit instead of the scary umbrella term?" (69 words instead of "learning English"; "a few minor outside-control adjustments" instead of "repairing your TV").
- For price reduction, always establish the higher anchor *first*, with a believable reason attached (overstock, special deal, bulk buy) — a price reduction stated without an anchor story reads as arbitrary and doesn't move the felt-value needle nearly as much.

## Worked Example
Full price-reduction sequence (Collier pattern) applied to spark plugs: "Up to now these extraordinary injectors were practically made by hand and would have to sell for as high as $5 each... 30 or 40 dollars was more than the average driver could afford — so we decided to get the price down so low... If you act now they are only $1.49 each." Every phrase in that sequence ("practically made by hand," "would have to sell for," "30 or 40 dollars," "get the price down so low," "astonishing proposition," "only $1.49 each") is a deliberate value/bargain-signal — roughly ten separate redefinition cues stacked in two short paragraphs, none of which required changing the actual $1.49 price.

## Key Takeaways
1. Diagnose the specific objection (too hard / too unimportant / too expensive) before choosing a redefinition technique — they aren't interchangeable.
2. Simplification works by naming the smallest actual unit of effort, not by asserting "it's easy."
3. Escalation works by widening the product's role in the reader's life, not by claiming it's "important."
4. Price reduction works by establishing a credible higher anchor first — never state a discount without a reason the higher price was ever real.
5. Manage "the turn" carefully: redefine the vocabulary *before* using the scary word, not after.
