# Chapter 4: 38 Ways to Strengthen Your Headline Once You Have Your Basic Idea

## Core Idea
Once you know *what* to say (from Ch. 1–3's strategy work), **verbalization** — the specific wording choice — is a separate creative act that can multiply a headline's impact without changing its content at all. This chapter is a toolbox of ways to *say* the same claim harder.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Verbalization**: "the art of increasing the impact of a headline by the way in which it is stated." Content is fixed by Ch. 1–3; verbalization is the final craft pass. It serves three purposes: strengthen the claim, make it fresh/new again, or pull the reader into the body copy.
- **The 38 techniques** (representative set — apply whichever fits the claim; don't force all of them into one headline):
  - **Measure it**: size ("20,000 filter traps"), speed ("in 2 seconds"), comparison ("6 times whiter"), how much work it does ("relief from all 5 troubles — in 12 seconds").
  - **Sensory-charge it**: metaphorize ("melts away ugly fat"), make them feel/see/hear/taste it ("tastes like you just picked it").
  - **Prove it**: demonstrate with a vivid example ("Jake LaMotta, 160-lb fighter, fails to flatten Mono paper cup"), dramatize the result ("They laughed when I sat down at the piano…"), tie in authority ("Boss mechanic shows how to…").
  - **Reframe it**: state as paradox ("How a bald-headed barber saved my hair"), remove a limitation ("shrinks hemorrhoids without surgery"), before-and-after it, condense it into a substitute image ("Pour yourself a new engine!" for an oil additive).
  - **Invite them in**: state as a question ("Who else wants a whiter wash — with no hard work?"), turn it into a challenge ("Which twin has the Toni?"), offer information ("How to win friends and influence people"), address the reader directly ("To the man who will settle for nothing less than the presidency of his firm").
  - **Signal news/scarcity**: stress newness ("Announcing! Now! …"), stress exclusivity ("Ours alone!", "Only Gleem has…").
  - **Break the frame**: accuse the claim of being too good ("Is it immoral to make money this easily?"), challenge a limiting belief ("You are twice as smart as you think"), turn claim into Q&A.

## Key Concepts
- **The three creative approaches, ranked by depth** (from Ch. 5, previewed here): word-substitution (weakest — swapping a competitor's proven headline formula word-for-word, becomes an "echo ad" that borrows no real strength unless product/market/timing genuinely match); formula application (medium — rules for *how* to phrase, valid but generic); analysis (strongest — starting fresh from the unique product-market-timing relationship every time).
- **Echo ads**: headlines copied from another product's proven formula without matching product, market, and timing. They fail because they borrow surface structure without the underlying strategic fit.

## Mental Models
- Treat this list as a **checklist to run after** you've locked the strategic content (desire + awareness + sophistication), never as a starting point. Picking a "measure it" or "sensory" technique before deciding *what claim* to make produces decoration without substance.
- Multiple techniques can stack in one headline — e.g. "SEE WHAT HAPPENS WHEN YOU CRUSH A HARTMAN DC-8? NOTHING!" combines demonstration + paradox + question in a single line.

## Worked Example
Take a flat, unverbalized claim: "This spark plug is more powerful." Run it through several techniques:
- Measure: "31 more horsepower, up to 8 more miles per gallon."
- Contradiction/startle: "'Hit hell out of the ball with your right hand,' says Tommy Armour" (borrowed structure for a different product — showing how the same *pattern* transfers).
- Redefine-as-substitute: "Now! A ring and piston job — in a tube!"
- Prevention-with-symbol: "Look, Mom! No cavities!" (result stated as a case-history quote, not a raw claim).

Each version says roughly the same underlying thing, but the verbalization changes which reader-reaction gets triggered — measurement builds credibility, paradox builds curiosity, substitution builds vivid contrast.

## Key Takeaways
1. Verbalization is separate from strategy — never substitute a clever phrasing trick for doing the Ch. 1–3 work first.
2. Measuring your claim (size/speed/comparison) is usually the single most reliable strengthening move.
3. Never build a headline by copying a competitor's proven formula word-for-word ("echo ads") unless your product/market/timing genuinely match theirs.
4. A headline can combine 2–3 techniques (question + paradox + demonstration) but should still express exactly one dominant desire (Ch. 1).
