# Chapter 7: The First Technique — Intensification

## Core Idea
Intensification is the technique of building desire by presenting *continually new images* of the product's satisfaction — never repeating the same picture, always adding a fresh angle, so the same underlying promise keeps landing as new information rather than repetition.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **13 Ways to Strengthen Desire** (apply as many as the space and the product legitimately support — stop the moment a new angle stops adding real freshness):
  1. **First presentation, direct and detailed** — the fullest, most thorough description of the product/result, stated bluntly.
  2. **Put the claims in action** — show, don't just tell, how the product performs in use.
  3. **Bring in the reader** — write "you," put the prospect directly inside the product-in-action scene.
  4. **Show him how to test the claims** — turn the demonstration into something the reader can verify themselves.
  5. **Stretch benefits out in time** — show the product working over weeks/months, not just the first use.
  6. **Bring in an audience** — testimonials, case histories, other people reacting to the product.
  7. **Show experts approving** — authority figures registering surprise/endorsement.
  8. **Compare, contrast, prove superiority** — direct side-by-side vs. the old way / competitor.
  9. **Picture the black side too** — the Heaven-or-Hell approach: paint the negative (what happens *without* the product) before the positive resolution.
  10. **Show how easy it is to get the benefits** — minimize perceived effort of acquiring the payoff.
  11. **Use metaphor, analogy, imagination** — personify the product, invent vivid comparisons.
  12. **Before you're done, summarize** — the **catalog technique**: a rapid-fire list either *horizontal* (many different applications/prospects) or *vertical* (many different depths of the same desire), used as a closing "shotgun" after the "rifle-shot" precision of the earlier copy.
  13. **Put your guarantee to work** — restate the whole claim set one final time, reinforced by the positive frame of a risk-reversal guarantee.
- **Campaign-level Intensification** (for repeated/national advertising, as opposed to one-shot mail order): compress the dominant image into a **Central Campaign Idea** stated in a fixed slogan or visual, then vary the *supporting execution* every insertion so the idea stays fresh without becoming unreadable through repetition. Three patterns: **Rule & Example** (fixed headline/tagline + constantly rotating illustration, e.g. "Which twin has the Toni?"), **fixed focal point** (background/typography/mood locked, e.g. Cadillac's consistent luxury photography), and **building-block ads** (each ad in the series reveals one new, self-contained fact about the product, e.g. Volkswagen's "Think Small" campaign — no single ad tells the whole story, but the series builds cumulative desire).

## Key Concepts
- **Reinforcement vs. repetition**: the same core promise stated in a genuinely new setting *reinforces* it; the same promise stated the same way *bores* the reader. The line between the two is whether the new paragraph offers a fresh angle real enough to justify re-engagement.
- **The Creative Gamble**: withholding the product name/brand from a headline in favor of pure curiosity/promise, betting that the body copy will do the selling job the headline chose not to attempt. High risk if the gamble fails to earn the read; high reward (2-3x effectiveness) if it succeeds.

## Mental Models
- Treat Intensification like a photographer walking around the same object — every new paragraph is a new camera angle on the *same* desire, not a new desire. If a paragraph doesn't offer a genuinely new angle, cut it; it will read as filler.
- For campaigns (not single ads), decide upfront: will continuity live in the *words* (fixed slogan, rotating visual) or in the *visual system* (fixed look, rotating copy fact)? Mixing both without discipline produces a campaign that feels different every time instead of cumulative.

## Worked Example
Schwartz's rose-plant mail-order ad demonstrates stacked Intensification: opens with the direct claim (technique 1 — "4,076 roses from one single plant"), moves into product-in-action ("picking 3 dozen roses a day"), brings in an audience of horticultural experts stunned by a comparative field test (technique 6+7), then closes with a catalog-style guarantee restating every benefit one final time (technique 13). Each paragraph is a different angle on the *same* single desire (abundant, effortless beauty) — never repeating the same sentence structure or image twice.

## Key Takeaways
1. Every new paragraph must add a genuinely fresh angle on the same one desire — never just repeat the previous claim in different words.
2. Use the catalog technique (horizontal or vertical) as a closing device, not an opening one — it works because it follows precision, not instead of it.
3. In national/repeat campaigns, lock either the words or the visual system as your continuity anchor, and let the other element rotate for freshness.
4. The Creative Gamble (withholding the brand from the headline) is legitimate only when the body copy is strong enough to close on its own — test this before betting on it.
