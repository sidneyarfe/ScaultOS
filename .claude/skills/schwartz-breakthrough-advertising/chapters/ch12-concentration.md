# Chapter 12: The Sixth Technique — Concentration

## Core Idea
Concentration is direct attack on the competition or on alternative ways of satisfying the desire — but with one non-negotiable rule: **never attack a weakness without simultaneously showing your product solves it.** Attack alone reads as biased and breeds skepticism; attack paired with the fix reads as a service to the reader.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Golden Rule of Concentration**: "Never attack a weakness unless you can provide the solution to that weakness at the same time... If you can show your customer that this attack is for *his own good*, then you have a sales story he will accept." An attack that only benefits the advertiser (disparagement) triggers distrust; an attack framed as protecting the reader triggers acceptance — even of the *positive* claims that follow.
- **Two Structural Patterns for Concentration copy**:
  1. **Point-by-point Bad/Good contrast** — alternate one competitor weakness with one of your product's matching strengths, repeated 5-8 times in a row, using parallel sentence structure and repeated/contrasted key words (e.g. "spark" vs. "fire") to sharpen the visual/verbal contrast at every pairing.
  2. **Before/after time-sequence contrast** — for desires with a recurring failure history (dieting, reducing plans people have tried and failed with repeatedly), narrate the reader's *own past experience* with the old, inadequate solution (redefining it as passive, hunger-only, will-power-dependent) before revealing the new mechanism as the active alternative. Uses "So what happened?" as the pivot from redefinition into case-history proof.
  - When to use: reach for Concentration once you dominate a category (protecting loyalty against competitors is unnecessary — silence is stronger) OR when you're the underdog needing to break an *existing* brand loyalty. Skip it entirely when your story is different/fresh enough that competitors have nothing comparable to be measured against — don't give them free publicity in your ad space.

## Key Concepts
- **Believability gate**: readers automatically know an advertiser's attack on competitors is self-interested. The *only* way to get past that automatic discount is to make the critique double as a genuine service — name the weakness, then immediately hand over the fix, so the reader experiences the paragraph as "being told something useful" rather than "being sold against a rival."
- **Alternative satisfaction, not just direct competitors**: Concentration also destroys *non-purchase* alternatives — old home remedies, doing nothing, will-power-only approaches — not just named competing brands.

## Mental Models
- Before writing any Concentration copy, ask: "if I deleted my product's name from this paragraph, would it still read as useful information, or does it collapse into pure disparagement?" If it collapses, add the solution before publishing.
- Choose your structure by desire-shape: if the weaknesses are discrete, parallel, itemizable features (mechanical products), use point-by-point Bad/Good. If the desire has an emotional history of repeated past failure (dieting, self-improvement), use the before/after time-sequence narrative instead — it lets the reader relive their own frustration before the new mechanism resolves it.

## Worked Example
The fire-injector spark-plug ad (also referenced in Ch. 11) is Schwartz's clearest point-by-point Concentration example — six consecutive Bad→Good pairs, each with matched sentence structure: "A spark plug accumulates filth and carbon... A fire injector never needs cleaning..." / "A spark plug gives you a thin, skimpy spark... A fire injector gives you a heavy, powerful flame..." Each pairing both attacks the old plug's specific weakness *and* immediately resolves it — never leaving the reader with a criticism unaccompanied by a fix.

The reducing-pill ad demonstrates the time-sequence pattern: it redefines "ordinary reducing plans" as passive hunger-suppressants ("all they could give you were HUNGER-APPEASING PRODUCTS"), narrates the reader's own remembered cycle of diet-then-relapse ("And then your will power snapped!... the fat flowed back"), then pivots with "So you tried another passive plan. And another. And then... perhaps you went to your doctor" — introducing the new active mechanism (lecithin) as the resolution to a failure story the reader already lived through.

## Key Takeaways
1. Never attack without immediately providing the fix in the same breath — bare disparagement backfires.
2. Frame every critique as a service to the reader's interest, not a jab at the competitor.
3. Use point-by-point Bad/Good structure for feature-comparable products; use before/after narrative structure for desires with an emotional history of repeated failure.
4. Concentration also targets non-purchase alternatives (doing nothing, home remedies), not only named competitors.
5. Skip Concentration when you dominate the category or when your offer is different enough that naming competitors only gives them free attention.
