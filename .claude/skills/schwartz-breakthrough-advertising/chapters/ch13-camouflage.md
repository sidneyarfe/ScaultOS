# Chapter 13: The Seventh Technique — Camouflage

## Core Idea
Readers trust the *medium* before they read a single word of the ad — they buy a publication because they already believe it tells the truth, and that trust unconsciously bleeds onto anything printed in its format and idiom. Camouflage borrows that stored-up belief by making the ad *look and sound* like the trusted editorial content around it.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Three Ways to Borrow Believability**:
  1. **Format** — match the publication's actual typographic conventions (headline type/case, subhead style, column width, illustration placement) so closely that the reader's mental gear-shift from "reading trusted content" to "reading an ad" is minimized. Schwartz's proof: the identical "How to Make Anybody Like You" ad, reset into the Wall Street Journal's own old-fashioned upper-and-lower-case headline style and left-aligned subheads, ran successfully 19 consecutive months versus a mediocre result for the generic magazine version — same copy, only the format changed.
  2. **Phraseology** — borrow the medium's own stock language and structural conventions: a news dateline and byline ("New York, N.Y. — A leading doctor today showed...") for print, or news-anchor cadence for broadcast, so the copy inherits the *reporting* tone instead of the *selling* tone.
  3. **Mood** — two specific non-"hard-sell" registers that bypass the reader's automatic ad-skepticism: **Understatement** (short sentences, few adjectives, self-deprecating whimsy — the Volkswagen "Think Small" style: "You never run out of air... Or hoses. Or water pump. Or rust.") and **Deadly Sincerity** (leaning over backward to admit a flaw or limitation before making any positive claim, so the reader trusts the praise that follows precisely because the ad admitted a weakness first: "This is John Horn's first book. He is not an accomplished writer, and it has flaws... But there are paragraphs in this book...").
  - When to use: whenever the target audience has strong, format-specific trust habits (a Wall Street Journal reader trusts WSJ-style typography; a skeptical, sophisticated audience trusts understated/self-critical copy more than superlative-heavy copy).

## Key Concepts
- **Conditioned reflex of trust**: repeated exposure to a publication's *style*, not just its content, trains readers to associate that visual/verbal pattern with truthfulness — independent of what's actually printed in it. Camouflage exploits this transferred trust deliberately.
- **The hard-sell stereotype**: ordinary "advertising language" (superlatives, exclamation points, hyperbole) triggers automatic reader skepticism purely by pattern-matching to "this is trying to sell me something." Understatement and Deadly Sincerity are the two documented escape routes from that reflexive discount.

## Mental Models
- Before adapting an ad's format, ask: "if I stripped the logo and product name, would this page be mistaken for the publication's own editorial content at a glance?" The closer the answer is to yes, the more stored trust you're borrowing.
- Deadly Sincerity works because *admitting a flaw is itself evidence of trustworthiness* — the reader reasons (unconsciously) "an ad willing to say something bad about itself must be telling the truth about the good parts too." Use it deliberately on your weakest or most-doubted claim, never on your strongest one (there's nothing to gain by "sincerely admitting" something already obviously true).

## Worked Example
Schwartz's A/B evidence for Format: the same book ad, generically laid out for a dozen shotgunned magazines, performed only "mildly." Rebuilt specifically to mirror the Wall Street Journal's own house style — upper-and-lower-case headline in Journal type (not bold/modern), a doubled old-fashioned subhead pair (a convention the Journal used but most papers had abandoned), subheads flush left rather than centered, and a plain line-drawing portrait instead of a glamour photo (matching the Journal's own editorial illustration style) — the adapted version ran monthly for 19 consecutive insertions with no drop-off in response. Nothing about the underlying sales argument changed; only the *camouflage* did.

## Key Takeaways
1. Format-matching a specific publication's house style is a measurable, high-leverage lever — often as impactful as a copy rewrite.
2. Borrow the medium's phraseology (news dateline, reporting tone) to inherit its truth-association, not just its layout.
3. Understatement (few adjectives, short sentences, self-kidding tone) escapes hard-sell skepticism for sophisticated audiences.
4. Deadly Sincerity — admitting a real flaw before making a claim — increases trust in everything that follows; use it on your weakest point, not your strongest.
5. Test whether your ad, stripped of logo/brand, could pass as the medium's own editorial content — that's the Camouflage benchmark.
