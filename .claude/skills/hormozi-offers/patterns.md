# Patterns — $100M Offers

## The Value Equation
**When to use**: evaluating or redesigning any offer's perceived value.
**How**: `Value = (Dream Outcome × Perceived Likelihood of Achievement) / (Time Delay × Effort & Sacrifice)`. Increase the top two, decrease the bottom two. Because it's division, shrinking the denominator toward zero has outsized leverage over inflating the numerator.
**Trade-offs**: the denominator (speed, ease) is harder and more competitive to improve than the numerator (bigger promises) — most competitors take the lazy top-side route, leaving denominator improvements as the more defensible differentiator.

## 5-Step Offer Construction Process
**When to use**: building a new Grand Slam Offer from scratch.
**How**: (1) Identify Dream Outcome — specific result, specific deadline. (2) List Problems — walk the customer's actual journey stage by stage, list every friction point using the four Value Equation buckets as a completeness check. (3) List Solutions — flip each problem into a "how to" statement. (4) Create Delivery Vehicles ("The How") — brainstorm broadly for every problem using the Product Delivery Cheat Codes. (5) Trim & Stack — cut low-value items, bundle survivors into named, individually-valued components.
**Trade-offs**: thorough execution (32-64 total problems is typical) takes real time up front; a short problem list is a red flag of under-development, not evidence of a simple market.

## Product Delivery Cheat Codes
**When to use**: Step 4 brainstorming stalls — need more delivery-vehicle variety for a given problem.
**How**: vary six axes — (1) personal attention level (1-on-1 / small group / one-to-many), (2) effort expected of client (DIY / DWY / DFY), (3) live delivery medium (in-person, phone, email, text, Zoom, chat), (4) recorded format (audio, video, written), (5) response speed (24/7, business hours, within N minutes/hours), (6) the 10x-to-1/10th test (what would you add at 10x price; how would you still deliver value at 1/10th price).
**Trade-offs**: none of these dictate what you'll actually build — the point is surfacing options before filtering, not committing to every variation generated.

## Trim & Stack Selection
**When to use**: narrowing a large delivery-vehicle brainstorm down to the final offer.
**How**: score every candidate against the four Value Equation questions (does it increase financial value, likelihood of success, ease, or speed?). Cut high-cost/low-value first, then low-cost/low-value. Keep only low-cost/high-value and high-cost/high-value items. Bundle survivors, name each, assign each a dollar value, sum to a total that dwarfs the actual price.
**Trade-offs**: prioritize one-to-many, build-once delivery vehicles — they yield the largest value-to-cost gap, at the cost of higher upfront creation effort.

## Sales-to-Fulfillment Sequencing ("Create flow, monetize flow, then add friction")
**When to use**: launching any brand-new offer, especially the first version.
**How**: over-deliver aggressively (even manually, unscalably) to prove the offer sells and generate cash flow + feedback. Only once demand is validated, use that cash flow to engineer leaner, more scalable fulfillment that preserves the same client-perceived value.
**Trade-offs**: early over-delivery is expensive/unscalable by design — the point is de-risking whether the offer sells at all before optimizing cost structure prematurely.

## Guarantee Math Check
**When to use**: deciding whether a stronger guarantee is worth the increased refund risk.
**How**: compare net sales (sales minus refunds) before/after. Example: 100 sales @ 5% refunds = 95 net vs. 130 sales @ 10% refunds = 117 net → 23% net gain despite refund rate doubling. A guarantee only nets negative if the refund-rate increase fully offsets the sales-conversion increase — rare in practice.
**Trade-offs**: match guarantee strength to fulfillment cost — unconditional guarantees suit low-cost/low-ticket offers; high-cost-to-fulfill or high-ticket offers should lean conditional or implied (performance-based) instead.

## Guarantee & Bonus Stacking
**When to use**: maximizing perceived certainty and value on a finished offer.
**How**: layer multiple guarantee types (e.g., unconditional 30-day refund + conditional 90-day "triple your money back") or sequential conditional guarantees tied to a timeline. Apply the same stacking logic to bonuses — present them one at a time (in 1-on-1 selling, after the first "yes," or after an objection to counter it specifically).
**Trade-offs**: never discount the core offer to close a deal — add a bonus or guarantee instead; discounting teaches prospects your price is negotiable, which permanently weakens pricing power.

## M-A-G-I-C Naming Formula
**When to use**: naming any offer, promotion, or individual bonus/sub-item.
**How**: combine 3-5 of — Magnetic reason why (Free, 88% off, Grand Opening), Avatar (hyper-specific target, e.g. "Bee Cave Dentists"), Goal (dream outcome as a word/phrase), Interval (duration, watch for platform compliance rules on quantifiable claims + duration), Container (Blueprint, Bootcamp, Sprint, Masterclass). Test 2-3 names as controls; iterate against the winner.
**Trade-offs**: using all five components usually makes names too long — brevity/punchiness beats completeness.

## Offer-Fatigue Change Order
**When to use**: response rate on a previously-working offer starts declining.
**How**: change levers in this strict order, cheapest/lightest first: (1) ad creative → (2) body copy → (3) headline/name → (4) offer duration → (5) free/discount enhancer → (6) monetization structure/pricing (last resort). Exhaust each lighter lever before moving down.
**Trade-offs**: the underlying value stack and fulfillment almost never need to change — treat structural changes as a rare, disruptive last resort, not a first response to fatigue.

## Niche Pricing Escalation
**When to use**: a generic offer is underperforming or priced too low to market profitably.
**How**: progressively narrow the target avatar for the same core product — generic ($19) → professional segment ($99) → high-earning sub-segment ($499, 25x) → hyper-specific avatar ($1,000-2,000+, 100x+). Same content, dramatically higher relevance and price.
**Trade-offs**: below ~$10M/yr revenue this almost always increases profit; beyond that, TAM (total addressable market) constraints may force broadening back out.

## Four Market-Selection Criteria
**When to use**: vetting a market before investing in offer construction.
**How**: score the target market on Massive Pain, Purchasing Power, Easy to Target, and Growing. A "bad" rating on any one caps the ceiling regardless of offer quality — a great offer cannot fully rescue a declining or unreachable market.
**Trade-offs**: a market can be "normal" (not spectacular) on all four and still support massive success if paired with a genuinely great offer — you don't need a rare, extraordinary market, just not a bad one.
