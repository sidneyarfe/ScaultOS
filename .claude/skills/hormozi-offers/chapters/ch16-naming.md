# Chapter 16: Enhancing the Offer — Naming

## Core Idea
"Having a Grand Slam Offer will not make you money if no one finds out about it." Naming ("the wrapper") determines response rate independent of the offer's actual content — the same underlying service, named well versus named generically, can see 2x-10x differences in response. Critically, when offers fatigue over time, you change the wrapper long before you ever touch the actual value stack underneath.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The M-A-G-I-C Naming Formula**: Magnetic reason why, Avatar, Goal, Interval, Container — five components, typically use 3-5 of them, not all.
  - When to use: naming any offer, promotion, program, or even individual bonus items within a stack.
  - How: **M — Magnetic "Reason Why"**: a word/phrase explaining why this promotion exists right now (Free, 88% off, Grand Opening, Anniversary, Halloween — the reason can be arbitrary as long as it's stated with conviction). **A — Avatar**: name the specific ideal client, as narrowly/locally as possible (not "Chicago," but "Hinsdale"; not "dentists," but "Bee Cave Dentists"). **G — Goal**: the prospect's dream outcome, as a word or phrase (Pain Free, Celebrity Smile, Double Your Profit, 7 Figure). **I — Interval**: the timeframe for results (4 Hour, 21 Day, 6 Week, 3 Month) — caution: many ad platforms disallow stating a quantifiable outcome (income, weight loss) together with a duration, since it implies a guarantee; safe to combine duration with a non-quantifiable goal instead. **C — Container**: a word signaling this is a structured system/bundle, not a commodity (Challenge, Blueprint, Bootcamp, Intensive, Masterclass, Sprint, Accelerator, Detox, Mastermind).
  - Style tips: shorter and punchier beats comprehensive; rhyme or alliteration help recall but shouldn't be forced ("Six-Pack Fast Track," "Debt Detox"); order of the components is flexible — use whatever reads punchiest.
- **The Offer-Fatigue Change Order**: an explicit sequence for what to vary as an offer's response rate declines over time, cheapest/lightest change first.
  - When to use: whenever conversion on a previously-working offer starts declining.
  - How, strictly in this order: (1) change the ad creative (images/video) → (2) change the body copy → (3) change the headline/name (the "wrapper," e.g. "Free 6-Week Lean Challenge" → "Free 6-Week Tone Challenge") → (4) change the offer duration → (5) change the free/discount enhancer → (6) change the monetization structure/pricing (last resort, most operationally disruptive). Exhaust each lighter-weight lever before moving to the next; the underlying value stack and fulfillment almost never need to change.

## Key Concepts
- **Implicit-egotism effect**: people are drawn to things/people that resemble themselves — the reason hyper-specific Avatar naming ("Rolling Hills Moms," "Retired Athletes") outconverts generic naming.
- **Changing the wrapper, not the offer**: the recurring discipline in this chapter — a renamed offer ("Six-Week Stress Release Challenge" → "42-Day Relaxing Holidays Challenge") is often the identical service underneath; renaming resets fatigue without touching fulfillment, pricing, or the value stack.
- **Local market fatigue**: because a brick-and-mortar's TAM is capped by geographic radius, a fixed ad budget can reach the entire addressable local population very cheaply (~$20 per 1,000 people) — meaning local offers fatigue faster than national ones and require more frequent wrapper variation.

## Mental Models
- **You are not changing the business, only its exterior perception**: naming/wrapper changes are cosmetic and reversible, unlike changes to monetization structure — treat them as the first, cheapest lever, and treat structural changes as a last resort reserved for when lighter changes stop working.
- **Test 2-3 names as controls, then iterate**: run several M-A-G-I-C candidates in parallel, keep the best performer as the control, and continue testing new names against it — Hormozi is explicit he can't predict in advance which name will "take off like a rocket."

## Worked Example
Wellness-industry names built from the formula: "Free Six-Week Lean-By-Halloween Challenge" (M: Free, G: Lean, I: Six-Week, C: Challenge, seasonal M tie-in: Halloween); "88% Off 12-Week Bikini Blueprint"; "60-Minute Make Your Friends Jealous Model Hair System." Doctor-industry names: "Lakeway Moms – $1,500 Off Your Kids' Braces" (A: Lakeway Moms, M: $1,500 off, G: implied via braces); "Back Sore No More! 90 Day Rapid Healing Intensive (81% off!)." Coaching-industry names: "5 Clients in 5 Days Blueprint"; "Fill Your Gym in 30 Days (Free!)" — each swaps in different M-A-G-I-C components while keeping the underlying offer structure identical across variations.

## Key Takeaways
1. Naming is not cosmetic decoration — it's a primary determinant of response rate, independent of offer quality.
2. Use 3-5 of the five M-A-G-I-C components, not all five — brevity beats completeness.
3. Hyper-local, hyper-specific Avatar naming consistently outconverts broad naming.
4. When conversion declines, change the cheapest lever first (creative → copy → name → duration → discount structure → pricing) — never restructure the actual offer as a first response to fatigue.
5. Name every bonus and sub-item in the stack (Ch14), not just the headline offer — it compounds perceived value throughout.
