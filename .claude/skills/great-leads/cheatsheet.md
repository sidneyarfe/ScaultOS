# Cheatsheet — Great Leads

## Decision Rules

- **Audience already knows and trusts you, wants what you sell** → Offer Lead. State the deal in the first lines; always include a "reason why."
- **Audience knows the product but is "on the fence"** → Promise Lead. Lead with the single biggest, truest, most original claim tied to a desire they already have.
- **Audience feels a frustration but hasn't connected it to your product** → Problem-Solution Lead. Validate the core emotion first, then pivot to the fix — don't linger past the point of earning trust.
- **You have a genuinely proprietary system, research finding, or mechanism** → Secret Lead. Name it in the headline, never explain it in the lead, escalate specific clues instead.
- **You have a startling, well-researched fact/prediction and a skeptical or totally unaware audience** → Proclamation Lead. State it boldly, prove it, return to it at the close.
- **Audience has no reason yet to trust a pitch, or the product needs emotional framing more than explanation** → Story Lead. Use the hero/doubt/triumph skeleton; keep the Golden Thread taut to the product.
- **A promotion is underperforming on a new/wider list** → don't rewrite the offer first. Re-run the Awareness diagnostic on the new audience, then re-pick the lead archetype. Real case studies show 3x gains from this alone.
- **Tempted to combine 3+ lead types in one opening** → stop. Rule of One says pick one dominant idea/emotion; permitted blends (Offer+Promise, Proclamation+Secret, Story+Secret/Proclamation) still serve a single core message.
- **A promise feels too big to be believable** → either turn it into a question ("Can You Really...?") or scale the specific number down until it's credible (the DieHard battery: 10 cars didn't work, 3-4 cars did).
- **Writing to a cold or over-pitched ("hyper-aware") list** → default to indirect even if surface awareness is high; jaded ≠ receptive.

## The Awareness → Lead-Type Map

| Awareness Level | Best Lead Type(s) | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Most Aware | Offer Lead | Only "the deal" is missing; education/trust already done |
| Product-Aware | Promise Lead | Knows the product, needs the big claim stated plainly + trust |
| Solution-Aware | Problem-Solution Lead | Has an outcome in mind, needs the specific fix connected to it |
| Problem-Aware | Problem-Solution Lead | Has only worry; needs pain validated ("I feel your pain") before any product talk |
| Completely Unaware | Secret / Proclamation / Story Lead | No frame of reference; needs curiosity or narrative to earn attention before any pitch signal |

## Thresholds & Defaults

- **80/20 rule of the lead**: ~80% of a promotion's emotional impact comes from the first 20% of the copy — spend disproportionate effort there.
- **Lead length**: typically 100-600 words in a long-form promotion — long enough to complete the emotional persuasion, no longer.
- **80% rule of real winners**: over 80% of documented successful promotions lead predominantly with ONE pure archetype (or one permitted two-archetype blend) — default to a pure type when in doubt.
- **When re-diagnosing an underperforming promotion for a new audience**: change the lead type before changing the offer.
- **A specific date attached to a prediction** (Proclamation Lead) measurably raises believability over an open-ended forecast.
- **Scaling a promise down** (fewer cars in the DieHard demo, "Get Rich Slow" vs. "Get Rich Quick") often outperforms scaling it up when the bigger claim risks disbelief.

## Tells & Smells

- **"I threw in every benefit just in case one lands"** → tossed-salad lead; violates the Rule of One. Pick the single strongest idea and cut the rest.
- **The secret/answer is explained in the first paragraph after the headline** → premature disclosure; the #1 beginner mistake in a Secret Lead. Rewrite to withhold longer.
- **A promotion "feels clever" but a test reader can't say what's for sale** → likely a failed indirect lead (too subtle) or a brand-style ad mistaken for direct response — check the Golden Thread.
- **A direct Offer/Promise Lead to a cold list keeps getting ignored or triggers pushback** → audience is less aware (or more jaded) than assumed; move indirect (Problem-Solution → Secret/Proclamation/Story).
- **A Story Lead is entertaining in isolation but nobody remembers the product/brand after reading** → Golden Thread has gone slack; the story needs a tighter loop back to the offer (the Dos Equis critique).
- **A bold claim reads as manufactured hype rather than shocking truth** → the Proclamation likely wasn't found through real research; go back to source documents/data before restating it more boldly.
- **Same offer, new list, suddenly underperforming** → don't blame the copy quality first; re-check whether the new list sits at a different awareness level than the original.
