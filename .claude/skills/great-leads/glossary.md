# Glossary — Great Leads

**Awareness Scale (Five Levels of Customer Awareness)** — Gene Schwartz's framework: Most Aware → Product-Aware → Solution-Aware → Problem-Aware → Completely Unaware. Determines both what to say and how directly to say it. (Ch3)

**Big Idea** — David Ogilvy's term for the single powerful concept underlying a great promotion; the historical precursor to the Rule of One. (Ch2)

**Completely Unaware** — the lowest awareness tier: the reader has no knowledge of the problem, the solution, or the seller. The toughest and potentially most lucrative segment, requiring maximum indirection (Story, Proclamation). (Ch3)

**Core emotion / point of maximum anxiety** — the deep, often unspoken feeling underneath a surface complaint (shame, fear of regret, loneliness) that a Problem-Solution Lead must target, not the surface symptom. (Ch7)

**Direct lead** — a lead that reveals the offer, product, or claim quickly, signaling clearly that the reader is being sold something. Works best on Most Aware/Product-Aware audiences. (Ch4)

**Directness Spectrum** — the operational translation of the Awareness Scale into a choice between direct and indirect lead construction. (Ch4)

**Dissolving bonus** — a bonus so valuable relative to the core offer that it "melts away" remaining purchase resistance. (Ch5)

**Dominant emotion lead** — Clayton Makepeace's term for an indirect lead built around a single strong feeling rather than a stated benefit or offer. (Ch4)

**Golden Thread** — the discipline of keeping every element of a promotion (including a story) connected to both the product and the prospect's heart; if the connection goes slack, the sale is lost. (Ch10, referenced Ch2)

**Great Idea criteria** — a strong lead idea must be big enough to stir interest, easy to understand, immediately convincing, and clearly useful to the reader. (Ch2)

**Indirect lead** — a lead that delays or omits revealing the product/claim, using a story, secret, or bold statement to build emotional connection first. Works best on Problem-Aware/Unaware audiences. (Ch4)

**Invitation lead** — a softer sub-type of the Offer Lead that trades explicit deal detail for exclusivity framing ("velvet rope" psychology); works only when the offer has genuine scarcity/prestige value. (Ch5)

**Most Aware** — the highest awareness tier: the reader already knows the product and only needs "the deal." House-list repeat buyers and brand loyalists. (Ch3)

**Neologizing** — inventing a new name for an existing concept to make it feel proprietary, exciting, or unfamiliar (e.g., ordinary dividend stocks renamed "The Chaffee Royalty Program"). (Ch2, Ch8)

**Offer Lead** — the most direct of the six archetypes; states the deal (price, guarantee, discount) in the headline or first lines. Best for Most Aware audiences. (Ch5)

**Pareto's Law (applied to copy)** — roughly 80% of a promotion's emotional impact is determined by the first 20% of the copy (the lead). (Ch1)

**Problem-Aware** — the awareness tier where the reader senses a problem but doesn't know a solution exists; needs the pain validated before any product mention. (Ch3)

**Problem-Solution Lead** — a lead that identifies and validates the reader's most emotionally-charged worry before pivoting to the fix. Best for Problem-Aware/Solution-Aware audiences. (Ch7)

**Proclamation Lead** — a lead built on a single, bold, confidently-stated declarative fact, opinion, or prediction, proven true before the pivot to product. Coined specifically by this book's authors. Best for Completely Unaware or highly skeptical audiences. (Ch9)

**Product-Aware** — the awareness tier where the reader knows what you sell but isn't sure it's right for them; needs trust-building, not education. (Ch3)

**Promise Lead** — a lead that opens with the product's single biggest, most desirable claim. Best for "on the fence" / Product-Aware audiences. (Ch6)

**Reason why** — an explicit explanation (scarcity, deadline, news event) for why a deal is being offered, included in an Offer Lead to preempt "what's the catch?" skepticism. (Ch5)

**"Reason-Why Advertising"** — John E. Kennedy's 1904 theory that ad copy should state the specific emotional reason a customer would buy, rather than merely announce or describe a product. Historical origin of the Promise Lead and Rosser Reeves' later USP framework. (Ch6)

**Rule of One** — the meta-principle underlying all six lead types: every strong lead is built around exactly one idea, one core emotion, one supporting story/fact, and one action — never a "tossed salad" of several. (Ch2)

**Secret Lead** — a lead that names and teases a withheld piece of proprietary knowledge, disclosing it only deep into the copy or after purchase. Works across categories (finance, health, gardening). (Ch8)

**Solution-Aware** — the awareness tier where the reader knows the outcome they want but not that your specific product delivers it. (Ch3)

**Story appeal** — placing a product or spokesperson inside an implied narrative/fictional context to give even a single image the emotional pull of a story, without literal plot text. Term borrowed by Ogilvy from researcher Harold Rudolph. (Ch10)

**Story Lead** — a lead that opens with narrative (hero, doubt, triumph) instead of a claim or offer. Argued to be the single most powerful archetype because it bypasses "I don't want to be sold" resistance. Can be direct or indirect. (Ch10)

**Tossed salad approach** — the failure pattern of cramming every possible benefit/feature into a lead, hoping one lands, instead of committing to a single idea. Violates the Rule of One. (Ch2)

**Transubstantiation** — elevating an ordinary claim or product feature by wrapping it in bigger, more dramatic historical/mythic framing (e.g., comparing a stock opportunity to the Industrial Revolution). Related to neologizing. (Ch2, Ch8, Ch10)

**Unique Selling Proposition (USP)** — Rosser Reeves' 3-part test for a promise: it must be true, original (competitors don't/can't claim it), and aimed at a desire the prospect already has. (Ch6)

**Velvet Pouch technique** — keeping a product or answer hidden while escalating description of its benefits, exploiting the reader's instinctive desire to see what's inside. The core mechanism of the Secret Lead. (Ch8)
