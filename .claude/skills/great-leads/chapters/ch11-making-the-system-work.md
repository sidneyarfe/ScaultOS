# Chapter 11: How to Make This System Work for You

## Core Idea
The six lead archetypes (Offer, Promise, Problem-Solution, Secret, Proclamation, Story) are not academic categories — real copywriters re-tested the SAME product with two different archetypes and got measurably different (often dramatically different) results, purely by re-diagnosing audience awareness and re-choosing the lead type. Roughly 80% of winning real-world promotions lead predominantly with one pure archetype (or a deliberate two-archetype blend); when in doubt, default to a pure type rather than a hybrid.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The re-diagnosis test** (the chapter's core operating procedure): when a promotion underperforms or a new audience segment is being targeted with an existing product, don't just rewrite headlines — re-run the Ch3 Awareness diagnostic on the *new* audience, then re-select the lead archetype from Ch4's directness spectrum. Several of the chapter's case studies are exactly this: the same offer, re-led with a different archetype for a wider/colder audience, outperforming the original.
- **Permitted blending, with a limit**: Offer and Promise Leads blend safely (they're structurally close and Ch5 explicitly permits it); Proclamation + Secret blend safely (bold claim → named system tease); Story + Secret/Proclamation blend safely (narrative opens, curiosity deepens mid-copy). But blending should still serve ONE core message (Ch2's Rule of One) — mixing for its own sake dilutes rather than strengthens.
- **The "muddy idea lead" failure pattern**: a test version that tries to combine a Proclamation-style hook with too many product benefits at once, forcing the copy to "work too hard" closing an open question before it can even start selling — documented losing by 50% against a clean, single-archetype Promise Lead in the book's own Karen Reddel case study.

## Key Concepts
- **The 80% rule**: across the authors' combined review of hundreds of real winning packages, over 80% led predominantly with one of the six pure archetypes (or a permitted blend of two) rather than an invented hybrid structure.
- **Re-audience, re-lead**: the specific two-step diagnostic (re-check Ch3 awareness → re-check Ch4 directness) to run whenever expanding a proven promotion to a new list or format.

## Mental Models
- **When in doubt, use a pure type, not a hybrid** — master each of the six archetypes individually first; only branch into deliberate blends once you've isolated a single sales message you're confident about.
- **The same lead-writing discipline (Rule of One, awareness-matching, directness-matching) applies beyond the lead itself** — to offers, guarantees, and reply-page copy throughout the whole promotion, not just the opening.

## Worked Example
**Angela Salerno's investment newsletter — Offer Lead → Problem-Solution rewrite**: original direct Offer Lead ("Why We're Giving You 12 FREE Months of...") to a house list broke $1 million in sales. When the same offer needed to reach a much wider, colder audience via a new format, she re-diagnosed the new audience as less aware, switched to a Problem-Solution Lead (leading with "the horrible mistake most investors are making right now" before the offer), and the same underlying deal became a $3 million letter — 3x, from lead-type re-diagnosis alone, same product, same core deal.

**Karen Reddel's heart supplement — Promise Lead beat Proclamation Lead by 50%**: A/B test between a clean big-benefit Promise Lead ("The world's most perfect heart supplement") and a "muddier" Proclamation-style idea lead (opening on "the medical mainstream may buy into the 'cholesterol myth'..."). The Promise Lead won by a 50% margin because the Proclamation version had to spend too much copy resolving its own open question before it could connect to the supplement's actual benefits — direct proof that audience awareness (supplement buyers already know roughly what they want) should have ruled out the more indirect archetype from the start.

**Aaron Gentzler's tech-stock letter — a nuanced Problem-Solution Lead** built entirely around the fear of missed historical opportunity ("Your Great-Grandfather Saw The Railroad Come to Town, But Did He Profit From It?") plus transubstantiation (elevating a stock tip into "generations-wide wealth") plus a Proclamation-style specific date ("The World Will Learn... on Nov. 19") — took in $500,000 in week one, sustained results afterward.

**Colm O'Dwyer's small-cap letter — a deliberate Secret Lead choice**: facing an already-strong existing control, Colm explicitly ran the Ch3/Ch4 diagnostic ("I knew that for the best chance at success, I should use a Story, Proclamation, or Secret Lead... this product just didn't fit a Proclamation Lead, so I decided to use a Secret Lead") — the resulting "Secret $200 Retirement Blueprint" beat the long-standing control and brought in 55,000+ new customers.

**Mike Palmer / Jim Amrhein's energy-market letter — Proclamation as credibility-builder**: "Just 50 People Control 90% of the World's Energy Markets... And They All Have Me on Speed Dial" — a bold Proclamation used specifically to establish the editor's insider credibility before any product claim, generating ~$6 million in sales in four months.

**Jennifer Stevens' travel product — Story Lead rescued a stalled offer**: a "retire overseas kit" that failed under two different direct approaches ("90 days to your new life overseas") was rewritten as a Story Lead following two real editors' decade-long personal journey (Ecuador → Mexico → Panama → Nicaragua → back to Mexico) — the narrative version generated $75,636 in five days where direct framing had failed twice.

## Key Takeaways
1. If a promotion underperforms with a new/wider audience, re-run the Ch3 awareness diagnostic before rewriting copy — the fix is often "wrong archetype for this audience," not "weak writing."
2. Blending archetypes is permitted (Offer+Promise, Proclamation+Secret, Story+Secret/Proclamation) but must still serve one core message — don't blend to hedge.
3. A/B test archetype choice itself, not just headline wording, when a category has a long-standing control — switching lead types can outperform switching words.
4. Specific dates, named systems, and personal-journey narratives are the recurring difference-makers across every real case study in this chapter — abstraction is the common failure pattern.
5. Master all six pure types individually before improvising hybrids — the 80% rule is empirical evidence that discipline beats invention here.
