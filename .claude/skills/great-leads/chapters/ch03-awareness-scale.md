# Chapter 3: The Simple Question That Can Double Your Success

## Core Idea
Before deciding what to say or how to say it, answer one question: "What does my customer already know?" Gene Schwartz's Five Levels of Awareness determine not just word choice but which of the six lead archetypes will work at all — the same product pitched to differently-aware audiences requires structurally different openings.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Gene Schwartz's Five Levels of Customer Awareness** (from *Breakthrough Advertising*), running Most Aware → Least Aware:
  1. **Most Aware** — knows your product, only needs "the deal" (repeat buyers, brand loyalists, house-list multi-buyers).
  2. **Product-Aware** — knows what you sell, isn't sure it's right for them (fence-sitters who need trust-building, not education).
  3. **Solution-Aware** — knows the outcome they want, not that your product delivers it (needs education before comparing options).
  4. **Problem-Aware** — senses a problem, doesn't know a solution exists (needs the "point of maximum anxiety" acknowledged before any product talk).
  5. **Completely Unaware** — no knowledge of the problem, the solution, or you; the toughest and most lucrative segment (new-market creation), requiring maximum finesse.
  - When to use: as the very first diagnostic question for any promotion, before choosing a lead type or writing a headline.
  - How: plot the *specific list or audience segment* you're mailing/targeting on this five-point scale — house list vs. cold list vs. rented list will usually sit at different points.
  - Why it works: Schwartz's own formulation — "If [the prospect] is aware of your product... your headline starts with your product. If not... starts with the desire. If not even that... starts with the problem and crystallizes it into a specific need." Awareness level dictates where the message must *begin*.
- **The Awareness-to-Directness rule** (the payoff of this chapter, expanded fully in Ch4): "The more aware he is, usually the more direct sales lead works best. The less aware, the more indirect you're going to want to go." — not a perfect rule, but the fastest first-pass filter for choosing a lead archetype.

## Key Concepts
- **Most Aware**: dream customer; only "the deal" is missing — Apple "fanboys" camping outside stores exemplify this tier.
- **Product-Aware**: knows the name and claimed benefits, still needs reassurance (reads Amazon reviews, Consumer Reports) — trust-building is the primary job here.
- **Solution-Aware**: has an outcome in mind (like standing in front of an open fridge, hungry, unsure what he wants) but no specific product in mind yet.
- **Problem-Aware**: only has worry, not hope; the "point of maximum anxiety" is the specific frustration/fear/desperation attached to the problem — identify it and speak to it before naming any product.
- **Completely Unaware**: no frame of reference for the problem, the category, or you — requires an entry point that doesn't reveal intent too early or the prospect disengages (used by Story and Proclamation Leads).
- **House list / multi-buyers**: direct marketing terms for a company's own list of past buyers, especially repeat buyers — the practical proxy for "Most Aware" when deciding a targeting strategy.

## Mental Models
- **"Hyper-aware" ≠ "Most Aware"**: a segment can be over-exposed to similar pitches, jaded, and have already made up its mind — a special sub-case that behaves more like resistant Problem-Aware than genuinely receptive Most Aware. Don't assume high familiarity always means high receptivity.
- **Detective questions to place a customer on the scale**: How new is the product to market? Is there anything else out there like it (a frame of reference)? These two questions alone usually locate a segment on the five-point scale.

## Worked Example
The chapter's closing example is *The Wall Street Journal*'s classic "tale of two young men" letter — deliberately targeting a Completely Unaware audience. It opens with pure narrative (two college friends, equally promising, reunite 25 years later — one is a department manager, the other company president) and never mentions the newspaper or a subscription offer anywhere in the lead. Because the target reader has no reason yet to trust or listen to a direct pitch, the letter earns attention through story alone before it ever reveals what's being sold — becoming one of the most successful and most-copied direct response letters in history (fully dissected in Ch10).

## Key Takeaways
1. Always ask "what does this specific list/segment already know?" before drafting — it changes everything downstream.
2. A single product can need six different leads for six differently-aware segments; there is no single "best" lead independent of audience.
3. Product-Aware and Solution-Aware readers need *trust and education*, not bigger promises — matching the wrong lead type here (e.g., a hard Offer Lead) reads as pushy and stalls the sale.
4. Problem-Aware readers must feel understood ("I feel your pain") before any product mention, or you risk being dismissed as tone-deaf.
5. Completely Unaware audiences represent the biggest growth opportunity (new markets, new product-line extensions) but demand the most disciplined, patient lead construction.
