# Chapter 4: Direct or Indirect?

## Core Idea
Every lead sits somewhere on a directness spectrum — how quickly it reveals that you're selling something. Direct leads (Offer, Promise) work because the reader already trusts you or wants what you're offering; indirect leads (Secret, Proclamation, Story) work because they suspend the reader's "I'm being sold" defenses long enough to build the emotional connection first. Choosing correctly is mostly a function of the Ch3 Awareness Scale, though five additional situational factors refine the choice.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Directness Spectrum**, mapped directly onto the Ch3 Awareness Scale: Most Aware/Product-Aware → Direct leads (Offer, Promise) work best. Problem-Aware/Unaware → Indirect leads (Secret, Proclamation, Story) work best. Solution-Aware and Problem-Aware sit in the middle (Problem-Solution Lead).
  - When to use: as the second diagnostic (after placing the audience on the Awareness Scale) — translates awareness level into a directness decision.
  - How: ask "does my customer already trust me, already want this, already know the deal is good?" If yes on all three → go direct. If no on any → move indirect until trust is built.
  - Why it works (military metaphor, Bill Bonner): direct leads are like a frontal assault — effective when you have superior force (existing trust/awareness). Indirect leads are like flanking maneuvers — effective when a frontal approach would be "outflanked and outmatched" by the reader's skepticism, time pressure, or unfamiliarity.
- **Five situations favoring Direct leads**: (1) product is easy to understand, (2) the promise is large and easily accepted, (3) an exceptionally good deal/guarantee exists, (4) the customer already knows and trusts you, (5) the product improvement is one the market was already waiting for.
- **Five situations favoring Indirect leads**: (1) writing to someone who knows little/nothing about you, (2) selling something that needs explanation, (3) facing a jaded/skeptical customer, (4) riding a timely news hook too big to ignore, (5) reinventing or elevating the product/idea.
- **Clayton Makepeace's "dominant emotion" leads**: his term for indirect leads built around a single strong emotion rather than a stated benefit — in his own testing, these have been winning more often in recent years and account for most of his biggest controls (20-30 million pieces/year).

## Key Concepts
- **Direct lead**: reveals product, price, deal details early — reader knows immediately he's reading a pitch.
- **Indirect lead**: opens with a story, secret, or bold statement that delays or omits direct product/claim mention — reader temporarily forgets he's reading an ad.
- **Dominant emotion lead**: Makepeace's synonym for indirect leads built to trigger one strong feeling before making any claim.

## Mental Models
- **Both directness camps are defensible at the top of the profession** — the chapter deliberately quotes conflicting expert opinions (Hauptman favoring indirect as "the wave of the future"; Bly favoring direct from years in B2B; Bonner favoring indirect because "it forces you to aim straight at the heart, the risks are higher, but so are the rewards") — the lesson is that context (audience awareness) decides, not personal style.
- **Direct-lead risk**: comes on too strong, invites instant dismissal before the reader even knows what's offered — happens when trust or awareness is lower than assumed.
- **Indirect-lead risks** (five explicit failure modes): using indirect when direct would suffice; picking a secret/story too disconnected from the product to loop back convincingly; being too subtle to ever get to the product; boring the reader by taking too long; getting seduced into writing something merely "interesting" but irrelevant to the sale.

## Worked Example
**TurboTax (Direct win)**: a "Try It Free" CD mailer that jumps straight to the offer — no emotional warm-up about tax-season pain — because the target (existing/former users) already knows the pain and the brand; the relationship is pre-built, so cutting to the irresistible free-trial offer is the strongest move.

**Cooking Club of America (Direct win, different mechanism)**: an envelope reading "WE'RE LOOKING FOR PEOPLE TO TEST COOKING PRODUCTS!" opens the letter with "Would you mind very much if I sent you a free cookware set...?" — a direct Invitation-style Offer Lead that skips all warm-up because the list (self-identified "serious cooks") is already passionate and informed; no education needed.

**"What Doctors Won't Tell You" health promo (Indirect win)**: opens with a testimonial-driven headline ("I had one foot in the grave... My doctor sent me to the nursing home to die... But what happened next might be called a miracle!") that reveals the health topic but nothing about the specific product — pulling in even skeptical/disconnected readers through curiosity before any claim is made.

## Key Takeaways
1. Directness is not a stylistic choice — it's a response to a measurable fact: how much the reader already trusts you and wants what you sell.
2. When in doubt with a jaded, skeptical, or cold list, default toward indirect — the downside of over-explaining is smaller than the downside of triggering "here's another sales pitch" dismissal.
3. Indirect leads take more work because you must "get inside your prospect's head" deeply enough to find a story/secret/statement relevant enough to loop back to the product — budget the extra research time.
4. Never confuse "clever" or "entertaining" with "successfully indirect" — most humor-based brand ads fail as direct-response leads because the reader never learns what's for sale.
5. This is a spectrum, not a binary — Story Leads especially (Ch10) can be written either directly or indirectly depending on the audience.
