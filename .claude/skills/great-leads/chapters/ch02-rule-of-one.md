# Chapter 2: The Rule of One — One Big Idea

## Core Idea
Every strong lead is driven by exactly ONE idea, ONE emotion, ONE story or fact, and points to ONE action. Mixing multiple ideas, benefits, or emotional appeals in a single lead ("the tossed salad approach") dilutes all of them — the reader's attention fragments across competing claims instead of committing to one.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Rule of One** (called "the Big Idea" by David Ogilvy; independently arrived at by the authors from analyzing their highest-rated *Early to Rise* essays): a lead/promotion is strongest when built around a single, narrow, deeply-developed idea rather than a list of several good-but-shallow ones.
  - When to use: at the concept stage of every lead, before writing a single sentence — force yourself to name the ONE idea first.
  - How: identify the single idea; identify the single core emotion it should trigger; support it with one story or compelling fact; point to one inevitable action. Reject or cut every element that doesn't serve that one idea.
  - Why it works: readers don't want everything you know about a topic — they want one useful, deeply-presented idea. A single strong idea, fully developed, beats a dozen so-so ideas strung together, because competing ideas pull the reader's attention (and emotional commitment) in different directions.
  - Failure mode ("the tossed salad approach"): throwing every feature/benefit into the lead hoping one will land — "I don't know which benefit will push the buttons, so I'll throw them all in." Signature of B-level copywriting; the fix is deciding *before* writing, not hedging *while* writing.
- **The Golden Thread** (referenced from the author's other work, used to bridge Rule of One to the whole promotion): the product sits at one end, the prospect's heart at the other; every element of copy must stay connected to both, without going slack, or the sale is lost.

## Key Concepts
- **Big Idea**: Ogilvy's term for the single powerful concept a great promotion is built on — "unless your advertising is built on a Big Idea, it will pass like a ship in the night."
- **Neologizing/transubstantiation** (introduced here, expanded in Ch8): turning something ordinary into something that *feels* special/new by renaming or reframing it — a technique for keeping a lead unified around one idea even while using multiple supporting devices (story + fact + promise).
- **Schwab's Top 100 Headlines finding**: of the 100 greatest headlines catalogued by Victor Schwab, 91 were driven by an explicit single idea; the remaining 9 still had one strong implied unifying theme.

## Mental Models
- **A lead can use multiple techniques (story + secret + emotional fact) and still obey the Rule of One** — the rule isn't "one technique," it's "one idea and one emotion that everything else supports." Porter Stansberry's "Railway package" used both a Story Lead and a Secret Lead, but the whole thing served a single idea: we're in an Industrial-Revolution-scale moment of change, and reading this letter is your ticket to it.
- **Test for violation**: if you can't state the lead's single core idea in one sentence, it's violating the rule — go back and cut.

## Worked Example
Bob Bly's e-book promotion (real ETR mailing) is dissected as a five-part textbook application of the Rule of One:
- One good idea: "There's no product easier to create or sell online than a simple, straightforward how-to e-book."
- One core emotion: "It is simple! I bet I can do it!"
- One captivating story, told in 17 words: "Would you be interested in investing $175 to make $20,727? That's exactly what Bob Bly just accomplished!"
- One single, desirable benefit: "show you how to make huge profits creating and selling simple e-books."
- One inevitable response: "click here now" to get the $19 price before it reverts to $59.

Contrast case (real ad taglines that violate vs. obey the rule): "The pause that refreshes" (Coca-Cola) works; "The pause that refreshes and always cool" would not. Same logic for "You deserve a break today" (McDonald's) vs. "You deserve a break today, and you're lovin' it."

## Key Takeaways
1. Decide the ONE idea before writing the first sentence — don't discover it by drafting and hoping to converge.
2. A lead is allowed to combine techniques (story, fact, secret) but only in service of one idea and one emotion — never to hedge between several ideas.
3. If you have six good reasons or twelve techniques, that's a signal to pick the single strongest one, not a reason to include them all.
4. The temptation to violate this rule usually comes from fear the reader will think "is that all he has to say?" — resist it; density beats breadth.
5. Use the Rule of One outside copy too — meetings, networking, daily goals — as a general focus discipline.
