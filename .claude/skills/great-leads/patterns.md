# Patterns — Great Leads

## The Rule of One (structural pattern for every lead)
**When to use**: at the concept stage of any lead, before writing a single sentence — regardless of which of the six archetypes you're using.
**How**: name the ONE idea; name the ONE core emotion it should trigger; support it with one story/fact; point to ONE action. Cut anything that doesn't serve that single idea, even if it's true and interesting.
**Trade-offs**: forces you to choose and discard good material — the temptation to hedge with "just in case this benefit doesn't land" must be resisted. A narrower, deeper lead reliably outperforms a broader, shallower one.

## The Awareness → Directness Decision Chain
**When to use**: before selecting which of the six lead archetypes to write, for every new promotion or new audience segment.
**How**: (1) place the specific list/segment on Schwartz's five-level Awareness Scale (Ch3); (2) translate that placement into a directness choice (Ch4) — Most/Product-Aware → direct (Offer/Promise); Solution/Problem-Aware → semi-indirect (Problem-Solution); Completely Unaware → indirect (Secret/Proclamation/Story).
**Trade-offs**: not a perfect rule — "hyper-aware" jaded segments can behave like resistant Problem-Aware readers despite high familiarity; always sanity-check with the five direct-favoring and five indirect-favoring situational factors from Ch4.

## The Offer Lead 4-Step Formula
**When to use**: Most Aware audiences (house lists, repeat buyers, self-identified enthusiasts) where trust and desire are already established.
**How**: (1) lead with the single most emotionally-compelling deal detail; (2) underscore the deal's biggest benefit; (3) elaborate that same benefit through the rest of the lead; (4) always include a "reason why" the deal exists.
**Trade-offs**: extremely easy to write correctly for the right audience, but fails badly (or worse than failing — triggers active resistance) on skeptical/over-pitched or unaware audiences.

## The USP 3-Part Test (Rosser Reeves)
**When to use**: to find what to promise before drafting a Promise Lead.
**How**: confirm the claim is (1) true — the product must actually deliver it; (2) original — competitors don't or can't make the same claim; (3) targeted at a desire the prospect already has (you cannot manufacture desire, only aim at it).
**Trade-offs**: a claim that passes all three tests but sounds too large risks disbelief — pair with the "claim too big" correction (turn it into a question, or scale it down until credible) from Ch6.

## The Problem-Solution 5-Step Formula
**When to use**: Problem-Aware/Solution-Aware audiences — readers who feel a frustration but haven't connected it to your product yet.
**How**: (1) identify the worry that actually keeps them up at night; (2) confirm it carries real emotional weight, not just surface annoyance; (3) validate/stir that emotion first; (4) don't linger past the point of earning trust; (5) deliver a relevant solution.
**Trade-offs**: requires real customer-language research (support tickets, forum posts, calls) to find the true "core emotion" rather than the obvious surface symptom — skipping this research produces generic, unconvincing problem-acknowledgment.

## The Secret Lead's Four Rules
**When to use**: proprietary systems, unique research, or any product where the mechanism itself is the differentiating story — works across finance, health, gardening, personal development.
**How**: (1) the secret must be both intriguing and beneficial, stated with a specific number/benefit; (2) introduce it in the headline; (3) never disclose it during the lead itself; (4) escalate specific, credible clues (named institutions, dates, dollar figures) as the copy progresses without ever giving the answer away.
**Trade-offs**: the single most common failure is disclosing the secret too early out of a misplaced sense of fairness to the reader — this instantly kills the reason to keep reading. Requires real research to "find," not invent, the underlying remarkable fact.

## Neologizing / Transubstantiation
**When to use**: when a product feature or category is well-known/mundane and needs to feel proprietary or new; or when you want to borrow the emotional weight of one concept (celebrity royalties) to elevate another (dividend stocks).
**How**: invent a distinctive name for the concept, or reframe it inside a bigger historical/mythic context (Industrial Revolution, plague, gold rush).
**Trade-offs**: must still connect back to something true and deliverable — over-dramatizing a modest product invites disappointment or distrust once the reveal happens.

## The Proclamation Lead's Four Rules
**When to use**: Completely Unaware or heavily-skeptical audiences; financial, political, health, or environmental topics where a genuinely remarkable fact or defensible prediction exists.
**How**: (1) make the statement bold, not hedged; (2) ensure it makes or implies a promise; (3) confirm the subject is relevant to something the reader already cares about; (4) return to the same proclamation's language at the close (guarantee/premium) to unify the piece.
**Trade-offs**: the "amazing fact" must come from real research (documents, studies, charts) — a manufactured-feeling shock claim reads as hype rather than as a startling truth, and undermines the proof that must follow.

## The Universal Story Lead Skeleton
**When to use**: any audience, but especially Completely Unaware readers who have no reason yet to trust a direct pitch; can be tuned direct or indirect.
**How**: (1) an ordinary hero, like the reader, attempts something extraordinary; (2) people doubt him; (3) he proves them wrong. Keep the Golden Thread taut — every story beat must still connect back to the product and the prospect's heart.
**Trade-offs**: the most powerful archetype for bypassing sales resistance, but the easiest to get wrong by letting the story wander from the product (entertaining but disconnected — see the Dos Equis critique in Ch10) — always end by asking "does this story loop back to what I'm selling?"

## Re-Audience, Re-Lead (diagnosing an underperforming promotion)
**When to use**: when a proven promotion underperforms with a new/wider/colder audience, or before scaling a winning package to a new list or format.
**How**: re-run the Ch3 Awareness diagnostic on the new audience (don't assume it matches the original); re-select the lead archetype from the Ch4 directness spectrum based on the new diagnosis; keep the underlying offer/product the same and rewrite only the lead.
**Trade-offs**: this often outperforms a full creative overhaul — several real case studies in Ch11 show 3x-plus gains from re-diagnosing the lead type alone, same product, same core deal.
